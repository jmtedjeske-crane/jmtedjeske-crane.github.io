Week 6: In-Class Exercise 2, Individual Prompt to Critique a Memo’s Use of Authority

Students complete this exercise during the sixth class, on the same memo as Exercise 1. Each student writes a prompt that asks Harvey, Copilot, or AI Studio to critique the memo’s selection and analysis of authority, runs the prompt, and then sorts two of the tool’s comments and decides what to do with each one. The exercise adapts Emily Pavuluri’s Memo Critique exercise and two of her handouts, which she releases under CC BY-NC 4.0. Purpose

In Step 10 of the Final Capstone Project, you will write your own prompt that asks an AI tool for feedback on your draft memo. Because this course is a research course, that prompt should focus on how the memo identifies, selects, and describes the authority that it cites. This exercise lets you practice writing that prompt on the memo from Exercise 1.

A general request, such as “How can I improve this memo?”, usually produces broad comments about writing. Your prompt today asks only about authority, and it asks about two things. The first is selection: did the memo choose the right authorities? The second is analysis: does the memo describe and apply each authority accurately?

An AI tool’s critique is advice, and it is not a supervising attorney’s review. After the tool answers, you review the reviewer. You decide which comments are useful, which comments are generic, and which comments you must verify before you rely on them.

Task

Copy the headings from the box at the bottom of this page, and paste them into the Canvas text box. Fill in the headings as you work through the three steps. Work alone, and submit your own answers. Your group meets after this exercise, and your group’s leader submits a separate group report.

1. Write your prompt

Write a prompt that asks an AI tool to critique the memo from Exercise 1 ([link to the memo]). Your prompt must tell the tool five things.

Tell the tool For this memo
The audience A supervising partner will read the memo.
The purpose The memo is an objective memo, so it predicts how the court is likely to rule. Name the court.
The level of feedback Say how many comments you want and how the tool should rank them. For example, ask for the three most important problems.
The focus Ask about authority only, and ask about both selection and analysis. Tell the tool not to comment on grammar, style, tone, or organization.
The limits Tell the tool not to invent facts and not to rewrite the memo. Ask for the comments as a table of suggested changes. Tell the tool to give a full citation for any authority that it suggests. Tell the tool to say, for each comment, whether you must verify the comment before you rely on it.

The questions below are examples of what a prompt about authority can ask. You do not need to use all of them. Choose the questions that matter most for this memo, and write them in your own words.

Selection: did the memo choose the right authorities?

  • Which cited authorities bind the court that will decide the issue, and which authorities are only persuasive?
  • Does the memo say so when it relies on persuasive authority?
  • Is there binding authority, more recent authority, or adverse authority that the memo does not cite?
  • Does any proposition in the memo rest on too little authority?

Analysis: does the memo describe and apply each authority accurately?

  • Does each citation support the specific sentence that it follows?
  • Does the memo state any holding more broadly than the court stated it?
  • Does the memo compare the facts of the cited cases with the client’s facts?
  • Does any conclusion claim more confidence than the cited authority supports?

2. Run your prompt

Run your prompt in the tool assigned to you: Harvey, Copilot, or AI Studio. Each member of your group uses a different tool, so your group can compare the advice that each tool gives. Do not use Lexis or Westlaw for this exercise, and do not upload cases from Westlaw or Lexis into these tools. Upload the memo if the tool accepts uploads. Otherwise, paste the text of the memo below your prompt. The memo is fictional, so it contains no confidential information.

3. Review the reviewer

First, paste the follow-up prompt below into the same conversation with the tool. The follow-up prompt asks the tool to break its critique into a table, with one claim in each row, and to leave two columns blank so that you make the decisions.

Break your critique into a table of separate claims. Give each claim its own row, even when one of your comments made more than one claim. Use these six columns:

  1. Number
  2. Where in the memo (the section heading, or a short quoted phrase)
  3. Your claim, in one sentence, using your earlier wording where you can
  4. The authority you relied on, with a full citation, or “None”
  5. Category (leave this column blank)
  6. Decision (leave this column blank)

Do not add new claims, and do not change or soften the claims you already made. Do not fill in columns 5 and 6, because I will decide them.

The tool leaves the Category and Decision columns blank because an AI tool that rates its own claims tends to rate most of them as useful. Column 4 shows you which claims rest on an authority and which claims rest on nothing.

Then choose two rows from the table. For each row, choose the category that fits the claim best.

Category What it means
Useful The comment identifies a real problem, and fixing the problem would improve the memo.
Generic The comment sounds reasonable, but the tool could have made it about almost any memo.
Legally inaccurate The comment misreads the law, an authority, the assignment, or the procedural posture.
Unsupported The comment gives no source or reason, so you cannot rely on it without checking.
Strategically unhelpful The change would make the memo read more smoothly, but it would weaken the analysis or serve the reader less well.
Needs verification The comment may be right, but you must confirm it against the authorities, the facts, or the assignment.

Next, decide what you would do with each claim: accept it, modify it, verify it first, or reject it. Give your reason in one sentence. You do not need to do the verifying today.

Be careful about letting an AI tool change your document directly. Ask the tool for a table of suggested changes or for tracked changes, so that you can see each change and decide whether to accept it.

To be extra safe, run a redline or review the tracked changes after extensive revisions. A redline compares the revised document with your earlier version, so it shows you any change that entered the document without your approval.

Time

Criteria

This exercise counts toward the In-Class Work component of your grade and toward class participation. I grade it pass/fail. A good faith effort is all that I require. A complete submission meets the two conditions below.

Your prompt. Your submission includes the prompt that you ran. The prompt tells the tool the five things in Step 1, and it asks about both the selection of authority and the analysis of authority.

Your review. Your submission names the tool that you used. For each of two claims from the follow-up table, your submission gives a category, a decision, and a reason.

Turn-in window You may turn in this assignment until [time] on [date].
If you miss class You still make up this work, even if your absence is excused. We will work out a deadline.
Scoring Pass = 95%. I score the In-Class Work component as a whole, not assignment by assignment. Reductions are expected to be rare, and you will usually, though not always, receive a warning first.
