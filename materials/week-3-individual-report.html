Week 3: In-Class Exercise, Individual Report

Students complete this individual report in the course’s learning management system during the third class, before the break. Everyone in the class checks the same short brief, and each small group checks three of the brief’s nine cases. Each student checks the group’s cases by hand against a five-item cite-check chart and compares what the student found with the report from one of four verification tools that the instructor ran on the brief before class. The group then meets to compare its members’ findings, and each student checks the report against an answer key. The page below shows [time], [date], [link], and [names] wherever a course-specific detail belongs.

Emily Pavuluri’s Red Line Challenge, from her open teaching project Teaching AI-Augmented Legal Research (CC BY-NC 4.0), is a similar exercise. In Pavuluri’s exercise, students check an appellate brief excerpt that contains planted errors typical of AI-generated legal work, and they log each error without the help of automated brief-checking tools. This exercise differs because it asks students to compare their hand check with the reports of four verification tools.

Where This Brief Came From

Julie Tedjeske Crane adapted the brief that students check from a real filing: the defendants’ brief in opposition to the plaintiff’s motion in limine in Coomer v. Lindell, a defamation case in the United States District Court for the District of Colorado. She changed the party names to Ostrander v. Vance. Judge Nina Y. Wang found that the original brief contained nearly thirty defective citations, including misquotations, misstated holdings, cases attributed to the wrong court, and citations to cases that do not exist. Order at 2–3, Coomer v. Lindell, No. 22-cv-01129-NYW-SBP (D. Colo. July 7, 2025), ECF No. 383, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-cod-1_22-cv-01129/pdf/USCOURTS-cod-1_22-cv-01129-10.pdf.

At a hearing, the defendants’ lead counsel told Judge Wang that his team had run the brief through generative AI and that he had not personally checked the citations. Id. at 4. Judge Wang then sanctioned lead counsel and his law firm $3,000 under Rule 11 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, and she sanctioned his co-counsel, who also signed the brief, a separate $3,000. Id. at 19–20.

Purpose

In this exercise, you verify the citations in a short brief in two ways: by hand and with a verification tool. By comparing what you find by hand with what the tool flags, you learn which errors software catches and which only a careful reader catches. You work as an Analyst in this exercise, because you test whether each case exists, says what the brief claims it says, and binds the court. You also act as the Decision-Maker, because you decide what goes in the final work product.

Everyone in the class checks the same brief. Each small group checks three of the brief’s nine cases. I have run the brief through four verification tools and posted each tool’s report, so you can compare what you find with what the tools found, and your group can compare how the four tools handled the same three cases.

1. Check your cases

For each of your group’s cases, read every page of the brief listed on the cite-check chart for that case. Then pull the case from Westlaw, Lexis, or Bloomberg Law and work through the five items on the chart:

  • Each citation to the case is correct, including short forms. Check the case name, reporter, volume, first page, court and year, subsequent history, and pin cites.
  • If the facts of the case are presented, they are presented properly. Is the brief’s characterization of the facts incorrect?
  • If the holding of the court is presented or implied, it is presented properly. Is the brief’s characterization of the holding incorrect? Check each explanatory parenthetical, and check that the brief relies on the holding rather than on dicta, a dissent, or a concurrence.
  • Case is still good law. Run KeyCite or Shepard’s and record the date you ran it.
  • Quotes verified. Check the wording, punctuation, alterations, ellipses, pin cite, and parentheticals such as “(quoting ...)” of each quotation. For a paraphrase, check that the pin cite is correct.

Tick Completed on the chart only after you have checked every page listed for that item, and use the Notes column for anything you found.

2. Use the tool reports

Read what each tool’s report says about your group’s cases. Check anything a tool flags manually: go to the case and decide whether the tool is right before you note it on the chart.

It is up to you how you allocate your time between using the AI-generated reports and hand verification.

3. Submit your report

You may upload your completed cite-check chart (Word or PDF), or paste the headings from the box at the bottom of this page into the Canvas text box and fill them in for each case. You do not need Bluebook form.

What Happens Next

After the break, your group meets for 10 minutes. Each member reports what they found, so that the group can compare its findings with the four tool reports on the same three cases. The group does not submit anything. After the group meeting, you will check your work against an answer key and submit a short follow-up.

Time

Checking and submission: 30 minutes.

Break: 10 minutes.

Group meeting: 10 minutes.

Answer key review and follow-up: 10 minutes.

Criteria

This report counts toward the In-Class Work component of your grade and toward class participation. I grade it pass/fail. A good faith effort is all that I require.

Turn-in window You may turn in this assignment until [time] on [date].
