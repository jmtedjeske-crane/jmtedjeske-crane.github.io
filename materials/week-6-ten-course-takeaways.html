Week 6: AI-Assisted Top 10 Course Takeaways

This page is an overview of the first six weeks of the course. An AI tool wrote the first draft from the slides, handouts, and assignments, and the instructor then reorganized the draft, added material, and edited it extensively. The page first describes the three roles of the human researcher, and it then lists ten takeaways, each with a short explanation. Students read the overview in the sixth class, before they write their own course takeaways. The page below shows a bracketed placeholder for the name of the AI tool.

Three roles: where you exercise professional judgment in the research process

The course uses a framework that identifies three roles for the human researcher. Each role marks a point in the research process where you exercise professional judgment and where you should not allow the AI tool to make a decision for you.

  • Strategist. You consider issues relevant to the research before using any tool. This is your research plan. You may revise that plan as your understanding changes. Other aspects of strategy include deciding what client information to give a tool and how you frame your prompt. Subcategory: Translator. Takeaways 1 through 3, and part of takeaway 7.
  • Analyst. You verify and evaluate the information and analysis provided by the tool. You read the output, check it against the authorities provided, and extend the research beyond the four corners of the AI output. Subcategory: Scout. Takeaways 4 through 8, and the first half of takeaway 9.
  • Decision-Maker. You determine what goes into the final work product. You select the authority, take ownership of the analysis, and stand behind the final product. Subcategory: Communicator. Takeaways 9 and 10.

!Remember, you are the researcher using AI as a tool. You are not doing quality control for AI-generated research.

When using AI in the research process, your goal, as always, is excellent work, not just avoiding sanctions.

Ten Takeaways

  1. An LLM predicts text. It does not look up an answer.
  2. The way you frame a prompt helps shape the answer you get.
  3. Protect client information before you type anything into a tool.
  4. Use AI output as a starting point for research, not as the answer.
  5. The concept of “hallucinations” includes more than cases that do not exist.
  6. Verification software helps, but a careful researcher still has to read the authority.
  7. Think of an AI research report as a bespoke but unstable and unreliable secondary source.
  8. Trace a claim to its original source, and check that source yourself.
  9. You identify the universe of relevant authority, choose which authority to rely on, and decide how much drafting to give the tool.
  10. An AI tool can review your draft, but you decide which of its suggestions to accept.

1. An LLM predicts text. It does not look up an answer.

In Week 1, we learned that a large language model (LLM) writes its answer one token at a time. The model chooses each token from patterns in its training data. We also learned that retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) grounds the model in documents from a database. RAG can still fail when the tool retrieves the wrong documents or misreads the right ones.

This point matters because an answer can read smoothly and still be wrong, so you cannot judge an AI answer by how confident it sounds.

As the Strategist, you use this knowledge to plan when to use a tool and how far to trust it.

2. The way you frame a prompt helps shape the answer you get.

  • In Week 1, we learned a four-part structure for a prompt, and you built a prompt for the Dating Disasters hypothetical. The four parts are:
    • Background and goal
    • Relevant facts
    • Desired output
    • Constraints

    Your group compared answers, and you saw that the tool that was used, as well as the framing of the legal issue in the prompt, affected the result. We also learned that a leading question or a false premise can push a tool toward a wrong answer.

  • In Week 2, we learned iterative prompting, in which follow-up prompts narrow, correct, or extend a tool’s first answer. We also practiced breaking a complex problem into separate research issues and using a separate prompt for each issue.

Framing helps shape the answer, but framing does not determine it. The exact same prompt can produce two different answers, particularly when you run the prompt at two different times. This variation comes from the way that LLMs work, which involves an element of probability. It does not necessarily mean that the tool found new information between runs.

Even though prompt engineering matters less than it used to, it is still important because a tool responds to the prompt that you gave it. Problems can arise when you ask the wrong question, give the tool incomplete information, or frame the question in a leading manner.

You are the Translator (subcategory of Strategist) because you translate your client’s problem into a question that the tool can answer. This process is similar to turning a client’s problem into a natural language query or a terms and connectors query on traditional electronic research platforms.

3. Protect client information before you type anything into a tool.

In Week 2, we learned that Model Rule 1.6(c) requires reasonable efforts to prevent disclosure of client information. ABA Formal Opinion 512 applies that duty to AI tools. In the client email exercise, you decided whether to leave out, generalize, or keep each of sixteen facts in a client email. The Week 2 handout gave three practical tips:

  • Give a tool only the facts that the task requires.
  • Learn which tools your organization approves, and for which purposes.
  • Record what you entered and what you checked.

This habit matters because you make the confidentiality decision before you send the prompt, and you cannot take the facts back afterward.

As the Strategist, you decide what information a tool has access to as part of planning the research.

4. Use AI output as a starting point for research, not as the answer.

In Week 1, we learned Rebecca Fordon’s idea of using AI as a toe-hold for legal research, and then we returned to the idea in Week 4.

A toe-hold gets your foot in the door, so to speak. Applied to AI, it means that you do not need to carefully review every authority and every assertion in the AI output. Instead, in a Toe-Hold Review, you record the output’s claims in a general manner, and you identify the most promising primary law research leads in the AI output. You then leave the AI tool and review those authorities yourself in Westlaw, Lexis, or Bloomberg Law, and you use them as starting points for further research.

The Toe-Hold Review rests on the idea that AI output can give you a general sense of the answer and the legal framework. The output can also generate research leads. You hold the AI’s output in your head lightly, and you do not use it to answer the research question.

You approach the AI output as an Analyst, meaning you evaluate it for leads and do not rely on it.

5. The concept of “hallucinations” includes more than cases that do not exist.

In Week 3, we learned that AI tools may invent quotations, overstate holdings, present persuasive authority as binding, and omit controlling authority. We discussed Damien Charlotin’s hallucinations database, which records court decisions worldwide that address hallucinations in legal proceedings. You verified the citations in Ostrander v. Vance, a fictional brief based on a real case, by hand while also using an AI tool’s report.

This lesson matters because ABA Formal Opinion 512 makes the lawyer fully responsible for the work, whatever tool produced it.

As the Analyst, you verify citations, quotations, holdings, and facts.

6. Verification software helps, but a careful researcher still has to read the authority.

In Week 3, we learned which errors an automated checker can catch and which categories of errors are best left to human review. The checkers include the more sophisticated brief analyzers and document analyzers. They also include programs that focus more narrowly on citator status and on checking quotations. In class, we used reports from Westlaw’s Litigation Document Analyzer and Lexis Document Analyzer, which fall in the former category. We also used reports from Lexis BriefCheck and two CoCounsel skills, which fall in the latter category.

A checker can report that a case does not exist or that a quotation does not appear in the opinion. At this point, the available technology is less able to determine whether a case supports the sentence that cites to it. This distinction matters because a clean software report can give you false assurance about a brief that still misstates the law.

Thus, as the Analyst, you can use software to help verify citations, but you need to be aware of the software’s limitations.

7. Think of an AI research report as a bespoke but unstable and unreliable secondary source.

An AI tool’s output is targeted at your facts and jurisdiction, but the answer can be wrong or incomplete, and it can change each time the prompt runs. In contrast, traditional secondary sources are written by humans, and they have usually gone through an editorial process. A traditional secondary source is designed to be read by many readers over a long time span. As a result, traditional secondary sources are more stable and usually more reliable. The drawback is that they are not as targeted at your legal research question as an AI output generated by a strong prompt.

Remember that for the Week 4 in-class exercise, we started with a research plan. Because you had thought about the issue first, you did not rely on either the AI output or the secondary source to frame the issue for you. You then compared and contrasted the research leads in the two sources, and you pulled leads from both.

As the Strategist, you plan before you open any tool. As the Analyst, you can use both AI tools and secondary sources, keeping the strengths and weaknesses of each in mind.

8. Trace a claim to its original source, and check that source yourself.

In Week 4, we stepped out of the traditional legal research framework to deal more broadly with research methodology. We learned Mike Caulfield’s SIFT method:

  • Stop.
  • Investigate the source.
  • Find better coverage.
  • Trace claims to the original context.

We also learned about lateral reading, in which a reader checks a claim against other sources before trusting the page that makes the claim. In the fact-check exercise, you used a chatbot to find where a claim came from, and then you left the chatbot and read the source yourself.

As I mentioned in class, lateral reading also explains the design of the Final Capstone Project. In the project, you read several AI research reports along with a traditional secondary source, so you see more than one analysis of the issue. When you read several analyses, one source is less likely to mislead you than when a single first source frames your whole analysis.

As the Analyst, you apply the same verification mindset to legal and non-legal sources.

9. You identify the universe of relevant authority, choose which authority to rely on, and decide how much drafting to give the tool.

Identifying. In Week 5, we learned that the authorities from the AI output and the secondary source are merely a starting point. We then reviewed the one good case method, which you may have learned in your 1L legal research class. (You build from what the case cites, from what cites the case, and from other sources on the same topic. You find sources on the same topic with Westlaw Key Numbers or Lexis topics and headnotes.) As the Scout (subcategory of Analyst), you go beyond your starting list of authority to build a more complete universe of potentially relevant materials.

Choosing. After you find the universe of potentially relevant authorities, you need to decide what subset of authority you will rely on to answer the legal research issue. To help with these decisions, we learned three questions to use in evaluating authority. Those questions are whether the authority binds the court, what it adds that no other authority adds, and which part of the analysis it supports.

Drafting. You compared memos that AI tools drafted in different ways, with access to different amounts of information. This comparison was meant to be a starting point for thinking about how much of the drafting a lawyer should give to a tool. We also discussed a trade-off. You can do the hard work of reading and analyzing the cases up front, or you can do that work afterward, when you verify an AI-generated draft.

These steps matter because selecting authority and analyzing that authority are core professional duties of lawyers. They also matter because you must be able to explain and defend your analysis in the final work product.

(Back to Top)

10. An AI tool can review your draft, but you decide which of its suggestions to accept.

In Week 6, we learned two ways to have a tool review a finished memo. First, you read reports from Westlaw’s Litigation Document Analyzer and Lexis Document Analyzer to help identify potentially relevant authority that the memo did not cite. Second, you wrote a prompt that asked a tool to critique the memo’s selection and analysis of authority. You then chose what to accept, modify, verify, or reject.

We also learned to ask for a table of suggested changes or for tracked changes. Either format lets you fully understand and control the edits made to the document.

As the Decision-Maker, you treat the tool’s comments as advice and make the final determinations yourself.
