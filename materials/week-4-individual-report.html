Week 4: In-Class Exercise 1, Individual Report

Students complete this individual report during the first exercise of the fourth class. Each student drafts a short research plan from a fact pattern about where a Rule 30(b)(6) deposition will take place, writes a toe-hold review of an AI tool’s output on the same question, revises that review after reading a secondary source, and then evaluates the two sources. I am not including links to the AI output or the secondary source that my students use, so the page below shows a bracketed placeholder for each one. To use the exercise, run the prompt described on the page in an AI tool of your choice, choose a secondary source on the same question, and replace the placeholders. The page also shows [date] and [time] wherever a course-specific detail belongs.

Purpose

In this exercise, you compare two starting points for the same research question: a report that an AI tool wrote, and a secondary source that a human author wrote. Before you open either source, you draft a short research plan, as today’s lecture recommends. You then write a toe-hold review of the AI output, as you did in earlier classes. Next, you read the secondary source and revise that same review, so that you finish with one review that holds the most useful authorities from both sources. Last, you judge which source gave you better research leads, and you assess where your own research stands.

You work as a Strategist when you draft the research plan, because you decide what the question is before any tool frames it for you. You then work as an Analyst, because you decide what each source says, which authorities look most important, and how much weight each source’s analysis deserves. I give you the AI output and the secondary source, so you do not write a prompt in this exercise. The exercise is a shorter version of Steps 1, 4, and 5 of the Final Capstone Project, in which you draft a research plan, write toe-hold reviews of AI outputs, review a secondary source, and compare the two.

Treat both sources as unverified

At this stage, you treat the information in each source as if it were correct, and you do not check the authorities. When this exercise asks which source gave what “appear” to be better research leads, it asks how the leads look on the page, because you have not yet confirmed that either source states the law correctly.

Task

Fact Pattern

You represent the defendant, a technology company headquartered in San Francisco, sued by the plaintiff, a Pennsylvania limited liability company, for breach of contract in the Middle District of Pennsylvania. Plaintiff has noticed the defendant’s Rule 30(b)(6) designee, who works near company headquarters, for a deposition in Harrisburg. Harrisburg is where the court sits, where counsel for both parties are located, and where plaintiff is headquartered. The designee travels regularly for business, including regular trips to Philadelphia. The defendant wants the deposition to take place at or near its corporate headquarters. Plaintiff will not move the location. Both sides have refused videoconference.

The partner wants an email memo: how will the court rule, and what do we file?

The prompt asked the tool for three things: a conclusion of two to three paragraphs; the controlling statutes, court rules, constitutional provisions, and regulations; and a table of authorities that gives a one-sentence description of how each authority applies.

1. Draft a research plan

Before you open the AI output or the secondary source, draft a research plan from the fact pattern alone, under the headings in the box at the bottom of this page. Do not use AI for this step. Short phrases are fine, and your first plan does not need to be complete. The plan has five parts:

  1. Issue: the issue to be researched, stated as one or more neutral questions.
  2. Jurisdiction (what law applies) and court.
  3. Key facts: only the facts that change the legal analysis.
  4. Procedural posture.
  5. Specific task: what the client or assigning attorney has specifically asked you to research or do.

(Back to Top)

2. Write a toe-hold review of the AI output

Read the AI output, and write a toe-hold review of it under the headings in the box at the bottom of this page. Do not open the secondary source until you finish this step. The review has three parts:

  • General Statement. In one to three sentences, state the output’s answer to the partner’s two questions: how the court is likely to rule on where the deposition takes place, and what the defendant should file.
  • Statutes and Court Rules. List no more than three statutes or court rules that the output cited and that look important.
  • Cases. List no more than five cases that the output cited and that you would pull next. For each case, give the case name and citation; say why the case matters (for example, Binding authority; States a legal test or definition; Closely matches the facts; or another reason that you name); and give a short description of the case.

More is not necessarily better, so list an item only when it looks important. You may copy and paste text from the AI output, but you may not use AI to write the review. You do not need Bluebook form.

(Back to Top)

3. Read the secondary source and revise your review

Open the secondary source. You do not need to read the whole source. Because the secondary source is a generally applicable resource that its author wrote for many readers and many fact patterns, you decide which parts of the source are potentially relevant to the partner’s questions, and you focus on those parts. Your research plan helps you decide, because the plan tells you which issue, jurisdiction, and facts matter. Deciding what to read in a secondary source is part of your research skills.

After you read those parts, edit the review that you wrote in Step 2. Do not start a second review, because you finish this step with one review that keeps the same three parts and the same limits: one to three sentences, no more than three statutes and court rules, and no more than five cases.

  • Revise the General Statement when the secondary source changes your view of how the court will rule or of what the defendant should file.
  • Add a statute, court rule, or case when the secondary source gives you a better lead than an item already on your review. When your review is already at the limit, drop or replace an item to make room for the new one.
  • Keep an item when the secondary source also cites it, or when you still think that the item is one of the best leads.
  • Tag every item. After each statute, court rule, and case, add a Source line that says where the item came from: AI output, Secondary source, or Both. Write “Both” only when both sources cite the item.

Leave your Step 1 research plan as you wrote it. Question (b) in Step 4 asks how the two sources changed your plan.

(Back to Top)

4. Evaluate the two sources

Answer the two questions below in complete sentences. A few sentences for Question (a) is all that I need, and Question (b) needs only a sentence or two.

  1. Research leads. Which source, the AI output or the secondary source, gave you what appear to be better research leads, and why? A research lead is a statute, court rule, or case that you would pull next.
  2. Temperature check. After you work with both sources, where does your research stand? Would you revise your research plan, for example by restating the issue or adding a key fact? Do you feel that you are on the right track? Would you need to do more preliminary work before you switch to primary law research? Are you confused about what the legal rule is, or about what the leading authority might be? You can answer in a sentence or two, and you do not need to answer all of these questions. I am looking for your general sense of confidence about where your research stands.

5. Submit your report

Copy the headings from the box at the bottom of this page, paste them into the Canvas text box, and fill in each heading. Submit your research plan, your one revised review, and your answers to the two questions.

Time

Research plan: 5 minutes.

Toe-hold review of the AI output: 10 minutes.

Reading the secondary source and revising your review: 10 minutes.

Evaluation and submission: 5 minutes.

Break: 10 minutes.

(Back to Top)

Criteria

This report counts toward the In-Class Work component of your grade and toward class participation. I grade it pass/fail. A good faith effort is all that I require. A complete report contains a research plan with an entry under each of its five headings. It also contains one review with a General Statement, no more than three statutes and court rules, and no more than five cases, with a Source line for each statute, court rule, and case. Last, a complete report answers both evaluation questions.
