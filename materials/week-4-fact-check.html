Week 4: In-Class Exercise 2, Fact-Check a Claim

Students complete this exercise during the second half of the fourth class, after a lecture on lateral reading and a demonstration of Mike Caulfield’s draft “SIFT for AI” method. Each student chooses one claim about law or policy, uses a chatbot to find where the claim came from, leaves the chatbot to check the original source, and writes a short reflection. Purpose

Beyond traditional legal research, you may find yourself needing to investigate non-legal sources related to policy issues or information relevant to legal representation. This exercise deals with verifying non-legal factual information found on the Internet. You practice Mike Caulfield’s draft “SIFT for AI” moves on one claim: you use a chatbot to find where the claim came from, and then you leave the chatbot to check the original source yourself.

You may not finish, and that is fine

Some claims lead to a paywall, a broken link, or a source that cites an earlier source. I grade this exercise on a good-faith attempt, not on whether you reached the original source.

Task

Work alone, in a general-purpose chatbot that can search the web, such as [list of chatbots available to students]. Follow the steps that I demonstrated in class.

1. Choose a claim

Choose one claim related to law or policy. You can choose your own claim or use one of the claims below. “What is the law on bail?” is a research question, not a claim. “New Jersey’s bail reform cut the jail population 44% with no increase in crime” is a claim.

Claims to choose from (or pick your own). Each of these figures circulates in news stories, press releases, or advocacy material.

  • Access to justice: 92% of low-income Americans can’t afford an attorney.
  • Legal AI: Legal AI tools hallucinate 17% to 33% of the time.
  • Data centers: Data centers could use 12% of U.S. electricity by 2028.
  • Recidivism: 83% of released prisoners are rearrested within 9 years.
  • Minimum wage: A $15 minimum wage would cost up to 3.7 million jobs.
  • Immigration: Undocumented immigrants pay higher tax rates than the top 1%.
  • Licensing: One in four Americans needs a government license to work.
  • Bail reform: New Jersey’s bail reform cut the jail population 44% with no increase in crime.

The wording here condenses each claim. Ask the chatbot where the claim circulates to find a real version, and trace that version.

2. Enter the claim into a chatbot

Paste the whole claim into the chatbot right away, without any instructions. Caulfield treats the chatbot’s first response as a first pass at the topic, so skim the first response, but do not rely on it.

3. Use at least one follow-up prompt

Use at least one of the follow-up prompts below, and review the results. Note the kinds of evidence that the chatbot reports, such as a survey, a study of a change in the law, an agency projection, or a news story.

To investigate the evidence, ask:

  • for the evidence for and against the claim in a table, with a rating of each item’s strength;
  • what the overall claim is, and what the evidence for and against it is; or
  • what a range of experts think, and how scholarly, professional, and news coverage differ.

To find better sources, ask:

  • for the best academic sources on the subject, with links, or for recent news stories or a general explainer; or
  • whether a study was funded by the industry that it studies.

I paraphrased these follow-up prompts from Caulfield’s March 2026 workshop page. For his exact wording, see Mike Caulfield, SIFT for AI (presentation, Let’s Talk Science Nat’l Day of Learning, Mar. 28, 2026), https://checkplease.neocities.org/ndol/.

4. Ask for the original source

Ask the chatbot for a link to the original source of the claim, or to the closest thing to the original source if the original is not available.

5. Leave the chatbot and check the source

Open the source yourself, if you can, and find the page, table, or paragraph that supports or contradicts the claim. Check three things: whether the source says what the chatbot says it says, what the figure measures (including its denominator and the year or years that the data describe), and whether the source cites an earlier source for the figure. When the source cites an earlier source, follow that citation too. The library’s databases, Google Scholar, and government agency websites can help you find the original.

6. Write a short reflection

Copy the headings from the box at the bottom of this page, paste them into the Canvas text box, and write a short statement on whether you found enough information to get a sense of where the claim originates and whether the claim has proper support. A few sentences is enough. You may include the topic that you searched in your reflection, but you don’t have to.

Time

Tracing the claim (Steps 1 through 5): 10 minutes.

Reflection (Step 6): 5 minutes.

Criteria

This reflection counts toward the In-Class Work component of your grade and toward class participation. I grade it pass/fail. A good faith effort is all that I require. A complete reflection says whether you found enough information to get a sense of where the claim originates and whether the claim has proper support.
